The recent blog post titled “Code was never the hard part” ignited a heated debate across developer forums. The author argued that large language models have made coding trivial and that the real difficulty lies elsewhere. Engineers and managers responded with concrete examples of non‑coding work that still dominates daily life. Recognizing this tension is crucial as AI tools reshape how software teams allocate effort.

<Callout type="takeaway">Coding may be easier with AI, but solving the right problem, coordinating teams, and maintaining quality remain the hardest work.</Callout>

## What happened
The blog post claimed that because AI can generate code, the act of writing code is no longer the bottleneck in software delivery. It questioned why programmers command high salaries, why classic texts like *Clean Code* and *The Pragmatic Programmer* exist, and why industry legends such as Carmack are celebrated if coding is “easy.”

A parallel discussion on Hacker News highlighted that most engineers spend the majority of their time communicating requirements, aligning cross‑functional partners, and planning architecture. One participant estimated roughly 80% of a software engineer’s role involves collaboration and strategic planning, with only about 10% dedicated to actual typing. The thread also noted that poor performance, feature stagnation, and rising bug counts often stem from non‑coding factors such as unclear priorities and misaligned incentives.

## Why it matters
If leaders internalize the “code is easy” narrative, they may underinvest in the skills and processes that actually drive product success—requirements gathering, system design, and stakeholder communication. This misallocation can lead to burnout, mis‑prioritized roadmaps, and a false sense of security about AI’s impact. Conversely, acknowledging the full spectrum of engineering work helps teams build realistic expectations, allocate resources wisely, and preserve the professional identity of developers.

<ProsCons>
  <Pros>
    <li>AI can accelerate routine coding tasks, freeing time for higher‑level problem solving.</li>
    <li>Highlighting non‑coding work encourages better investment in communication and design skills.</li>
    <li>The debate raises awareness of how AI will reshape engineering roles.</li>
  </Pros>
  <Cons>
    <li>Oversimplifying the claim risks devaluing deep technical expertise.</li>
    <li>It may lead organizations to cut training or mentorship programs.</li>
    <li>Developers could feel their core identity is being dismissed, harming morale.</li>
  </Cons>

## How to think about it
Treat AI as a productivity assistant, not a replacement for the engineering judgment that defines a successful product. Prioritize time for requirement validation, architecture reviews, and post‑release monitoring. When evaluating team performance, measure outcomes such as delivery predictability, defect rates, and stakeholder satisfaction—not just lines of code generated.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that reducing coding effort automatically improves project velocity; without strong planning and communication, faster code can amplify existing problems.</Callout>
