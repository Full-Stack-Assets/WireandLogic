A new research paper titled "8 Myths on Software Engineering and GenAI" was released this week, summarizing eight persistent misconceptions about AI in software development. The authors combined large‑scale studies, developer interviews, and field observations to quantify how developers actually spend their time and how AI tools affect workflow. Their findings show that the hype around AI‑generated code often overshadows deeper systemic issues. Understanding these myths is crucial for engineering leaders who want to set realistic expectations and measure true ROI. The paper also offers a framework for evaluating AI adoption beyond headline metrics.

<Callout type="takeaway">AI’s impact is limited by workflow context, not by the raw power of the models.</Callout>

## What happened
The authors report that developers spend only 14% of their workday writing code, a figure that aligns with earlier studies reporting an 11%‑18% range. The remaining time is divided among design, meetings, code review, coordination, and administrative tasks, meaning AI‑as‑code‑generator can only influence a small slice of daily activity.

A 2025 study cited in the paper shows that organizations adopting internal AI coding agents experience a modest 7.8% increase in overall code throughput. However, only about half of the pull requests generated by the AI agent are ultimately accepted, while 15% are abandoned and another 15% stall awaiting human review, highlighting bottlenecks that shift rather than disappear.

The research also warns against measuring AI impact by lines of code. As Bill Gates famously said, "Measuring software productivity by lines of code is like measuring progress on an airplane by how much it weighs," underscoring the danger of volume‑based metrics.

## Why it matters
When leaders base AI investment decisions on inflated expectations, they risk misallocating resources and overlooking the real friction points in the development pipeline. Metrics that focus on code quantity can incentivize wasteful practices, while ignoring design, collaboration, and review phases where most developer time is spent. Teams that understand the myths can align AI tools with actual workflow bottlenecks, leading to more sustainable productivity gains and clearer ROI.

## How to think about it
Start by mapping where developers actually spend their time and identify non‑coding phases that cause the most delay. Deploy AI tools as assistants for those specific phases—such as automated test generation or documentation drafting—rather than as wholesale code writers. Replace line‑of‑code targets with outcome‑based metrics like cycle‑time reduction, defect rate improvement, or reviewer workload balance. Regularly audit AI‑generated contributions for quality and acceptance rates, and adjust tooling or processes when abandonment or waiting rates rise.

<Callout type="warning">Do not use lines of code as the primary metric for AI productivity; it obscures real value and can incentivize low‑quality output.</Callout>
