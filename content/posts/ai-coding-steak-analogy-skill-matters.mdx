AI‑powered code generators have become as ubiquitous as a hot pan for a steak, promising fast, repeatable results. Recent blog posts and industry reports describe developers prompting models to write entire features, even from a phone, while the output can range from perfectly seasoned to burnt. This shift raises a crucial question: does the convenience of AI cooking replace the chef’s skill, or simply change the role of the developer? Understanding the limits of AI‑generated code is essential for teams that want speed without sacrificing reliability.

<Callout type="takeaway">AI can automate the recipe, but only skilled chefs can consistently serve a perfect steak.</Callout>

## What happened

The blog "Almost No Skill Required to Cook a Steak" likens AI‑assisted development to throwing a steak in a pan: the model follows a recipe, flips at the right moment, and can produce code at scale, but it often lacks the nuanced judgment needed for a consistently good product. The author notes that while AI can generate technically correct snippets, the results vary wildly—sometimes delivering a polished feature, other times serving "charcoal with a sprig of thyme".

A Forbes article highlights industry anxiety: a 2025 Federal Reserve Bank of New York study shows fresh CS graduates facing 6.1%–7.5% unemployment, double that of many non‑tech majors. Leaders at Anthropic predict models could perform end‑to‑end engineering within a year, prompting concerns that the traditional software‑engineer role may disappear.

Meanwhile, a Pragmatic Engineer newsletter recounts a developer who built production‑ready features from a phone using Claude Code, Opus 4.5, and GPT‑5.2. By prompting, reviewing tests, and merging PRs on the go, the author experienced a tangible productivity boost, illustrating how AI can accelerate certain workflows when the surrounding process is well‑engineered.

## Why it matters

The analogy underscores a shifting skill set. Speed and scale are attractive, but inconsistent quality threatens reliability, security, and long‑term maintainability. Teams that over‑rely on AI risk accumulating "AI slop"—code that passes tests but harbors hidden bugs or architectural debt. At the same time, developers who master prompt engineering, test design, and model limitations can leverage AI as a force multiplier, staying relevant in a market where pure coding is becoming commoditized.

## How to think about it

Treat AI as a highly capable assistant, not a replacement. Start by defining clear, test‑driven specifications before prompting. Use the model to generate drafts, then rigorously review, run static analysis, and add unit/integration tests. Keep sharpening core engineering skills—architecture, debugging, performance tuning—because those are the aspects models can’t reliably replicate. Finally, embed prompt‑engineering practices into your workflow: iterate on prompts, capture successful patterns, and share them within the team.

<Callout type="warning">Never ship AI‑generated code without thorough review and testing; the model can’t understand your product’s intent.</Callout>
