AI‑generated text is now routinely marked with invisible watermarks. Since August 2026 Claude models and earlier Google Gemini have been embedding these marks at the model level. The watermark lives not in hidden characters but in the statistical pattern of word choices made during generation. By subtly nudging the sampler toward a secret‑keyed “green” list, the system creates a fingerprint that survives copy‑paste. Understanding this mechanism is crucial for developers building detection tools or considering provenance requirements.

<Callout type="takeaway">AI watermarks are tiny, key‑driven biases in token selection that leave a detectable statistical fingerprint without altering the visible text.</Callout>

## What happened
The core idea is that at each generation step the model produces a shortlist of viable tokens, similar to an autocomplete menu. A secret key partitions that shortlist into a green and a red group; the sampler is then nudged slightly toward green tokens. The nudge is mild—red tokens can still be chosen—so the surface text remains unchanged.

Google’s SynthID implements the partition with a tournament that scores candidates using the key, while Claude’s approach colors tokens based on a short run of preceding words, meaning the same token can be green in one context and red in another. Because the coloring depends on recent context, the pattern is distributed across hundreds of forks in a paragraph.

Both schemes have been deployed in production: Gemini’s API has exposed a documented exception since 2024, and Claude models began marking output at the model level in August 2026. The marks survive copying text out of a chat window but disappear when the text is paraphrased.

## Why it matters
Watermarks give content creators and platform operators a way to prove provenance of AI‑generated text, which is valuable for attribution, policy enforcement, and anti‑misinformation efforts. Because the fingerprint survives copy‑paste, downstream applications can still verify origin even after basic editing. However, the reliance on statistical patterns means short excerpts may yield unreliable detection, and aggressive paraphrasing can erase the signal entirely.

## How to think about it
When designing a system that relies on watermarks, treat the signal as a probabilistic cue rather than a guarantee. Combine watermark detection with other signals such as metadata, usage logs, or stylometric analysis to improve confidence. If you need to audit large volumes of text, use open‑source detectors that implement the green‑list test and calibrate thresholds on your own data. Finally, stay aware of the legal and ethical implications of attempting to strip or forge watermarks.

<Callout type="warning">Do not treat a detected watermark as absolute proof of AI authorship; false positives are possible on short or highly repetitive text.</Callout>
