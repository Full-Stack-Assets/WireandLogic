Independent booksellers across the UK and beyond have suddenly been hit with massive bulk orders that dwarf their usual weekly sales. Stuart Manley of Barter Books, who normally moves two to three thousand titles a week, recently fulfilled a single Canadian order equal to a full week's stock. The surge coincides with a 2025 US court decision that cleared AI firms to train models on purchased books without copyright infringement. If AI training is driving the demand, the implications for the rare‑book market and copyright norms could be profound.

<Callout type="takeaway">AI-driven data harvesting is likely fueling the unexpected boom in secondhand book sales.</Callout>

## What happened
Booksellers from Barter Books in Northumberland to Walden Books in north London have reported unusually large orders that far exceed typical weekly volumes. Manley said he "never seen the like of this after 30 years in the second‑hand book trade" and noted a single bulk shipment from a Canadian company matched what he would normally sell in seven days. Similar reports have surfaced worldwide, with sellers unsure of the final destination for the titles.

The phenomenon aligns with a 2025 US court ruling by Judge William Alsup that deemed the use of purchased books for AI training "exceedingly transformative" and therefore lawful. The case involved Anthropic, whose internal documents labeled the effort "Project Panama" and revealed that books were being ingested—and in some instances destroyed—to train the Claude chatbot. Anthropic’s spokesperson emphasized that buying and destroying rare books is not a standard industry practice, yet the court’s decision has opened the door for large‑scale acquisition of printed works.

## Why it matters
If AI firms are the primary buyers, the surge could reshape the economics of secondhand markets, driving up prices for bulk purchases while depleting inventories of rare and out‑of‑print titles. Copyright stakeholders worry that mass digitization may sideline authors’ rights and erode the cultural value of physical books. Conversely, the influx of revenue offers struggling independent shops a lifeline, albeit one that may come at the cost of losing unique collections.

## How to think about it
Booksellers should audit bulk orders for provenance and, where possible, negotiate licensing terms that compensate authors. Diversifying sales channels—online listings, local events, and subscription models—can reduce reliance on single large purchasers. Monitoring legal developments around AI training data will help shops anticipate regulatory shifts and protect their inventory.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume every bulk order is for AI training; some may still be legitimate library or collector purchases, and mislabeling could damage relationships.</Callout>
