A buyer scheduled a showing for a property governed by a homeowners association (HOA). Before the appointment, the buyer ran a scan with Deflock, a tool that maps external camera deployments. Deflock displayed two cameras that the HOA had installed on the community’s common areas. Concerned about being recorded, the buyer canceled the showing altogether. This incident underscores how emerging visibility tools can influence real‑estate decisions and raise privacy questions for developers and builders.

<Callout type="takeaway">Deflock’s camera discovery can directly affect property transactions by surfacing hidden surveillance. </Callout>

## What happened
The prospective buyer used Deflock to audit the property’s external surveillance setup. Deflock’s output listed two active cameras linked to the HOA’s security system. The buyer shared the findings with the listing agent and decided not to proceed with the showing, citing privacy concerns.

The HOA confirmed that the cameras were installed for community safety, but did not disclose their exact locations publicly. No further negotiations took place, and the listing was temporarily withdrawn while the seller considered how to address the buyer’s concerns.

## Why it matters
For developers and builders, the episode illustrates that surveillance infrastructure is now a visible factor in the buyer’s decision‑making process. Privacy‑aware buyers may request documentation, camera removal, or masking before committing to a purchase. HOAs that rely on security cameras must balance safety with transparency, especially as tools like Deflock make camera placements easier to discover.

The broader real‑estate market may see increased scrutiny of surveillance practices, prompting sellers to proactively disclose camera locations or offer privacy‑focused upgrades. Ignoring this trend could lead to lost sales and reputational risk for communities that prioritize security without clear communication.

## How to think about it
Treat camera visibility as a checklist item in every property listing. Conduct an internal audit of all external surveillance devices before marketing a home, and prepare a concise disclosure that explains each camera’s purpose, field of view, and data handling practices. If privacy is a selling point for your target market, consider offering camera shielding, signage, or opt‑out zones. Communicate openly with the HOA to align security goals with buyer expectations, and be ready to adjust camera placement or provide alternative security measures when warranted.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that a tool like Deflock captures every hidden camera; conduct a physical walkthrough to verify the audit results. </Callout>
