Recent research by Raj Chetty and collaborators analyzed 21 billion Facebook friendships among 72 million Americans to quantify “economic connectedness”—the share of a person’s friends who earn more than they do. The study found that children who grow up with high economic connectedness earn roughly 20 percent more as adults than comparable peers with low connectedness. This effect rivals the income boost associated with a $20,000 increase in parental earnings and exceeds the influence of school quality, segregation, or neighborhood poverty rates. The findings suggest that who you know can be a more powerful lever for upward mobility than how hard you work or how lucky you get.

<Callout type="takeaway">Economic connectedness is the strongest predictor of future earnings, dwarfing hard work and luck.</Callout>

## What happened
The team combined 21 billion Facebook friendship links with tax‑return data to calculate each person’s economic connectedness, defined as the proportion of friends whose incomes exceed the individual’s own. Across the sample, children in the top quartile of connectedness earned about 20 percent more as adults than those in the bottom quartile, after controlling for family income, school quality, and neighborhood characteristics.

A separate analysis of job‑placement patterns showed that weak ties—acquaintances rather than close friends—were the most effective channels for upward mobility. A 2022 randomized experiment on 20 million LinkedIn users confirmed that algorithmic suggestions of moderately weak contacts increased job transitions and earnings.

These patterns held across income groups: low‑income children with high‑income friends earned substantially more, while high‑income individuals tended to cluster with peers, reinforcing existing advantage.

## Why it matters
If who you know drives earnings more than traditional levers, policies that foster cross‑income networking could reshape mobility pathways. Schools, nonprofits, and employers can design mentorship or peer‑pairing programs that deliberately mix socioeconomic backgrounds, leveraging the “weak‑tie” effect. For individuals, investing time in expanding modest connections may yield higher returns than additional hours of hard work alone.

## How to think about it
Treat economic connectedness as a strategic asset. Start by mapping your current network to identify gaps in income diversity. Seek out mentorship programs, alumni groups, or industry meet‑ups that bring together people from different economic backgrounds. When using platforms like LinkedIn, accept connection suggestions that extend beyond close friends, and engage in brief, purposeful interactions (e.g., informational interviews). For organizations, embed cross‑income pairing into onboarding, internship, and community‑outreach initiatives, measuring outcomes with longitudinal earnings data where possible.

<Callout type="warning">The findings are based on observational data from a single social‑media platform; results may not generalize to populations without Facebook presence, and privacy considerations limit data granularity.</Callout>
