Meta’s AI‑powered smart glasses hit the headlines this week as users and influencers label them “pervert glasses.” Viral videos show people disabling the built‑in recording light to capture footage of strangers in gyms, clubs and even private spaces. The backlash has prompted bans in Seattle cafés, courtrooms and several universities, and has already cost some creators thousands of followers. For developers, the controversy highlights how privacy‑first design can make or break emerging AR hardware.

<Callout type="takeaway">The privacy backlash threatens adoption of Meta’s smart glasses and forces the company to rethink its safety design.</Callout>

## What happened
Meta released AI‑enabled smart glasses that combine a camera, voice assistant and audio playback in a form factor that looks like ordinary spectacles. Early adopters praised the hands‑free interaction, but a wave of prank videos revealed users drilling out the flashing white recording indicator and using the device to film people without consent in gyms, clubs and even bedrooms.

In response, several public venues—including Seattle coffee shops, university campuses and courtrooms—have banned the glasses outright. Meta announced software updates that re‑enable the indicator by default and added remote‑disable controls for enterprise customers. At the same time, influencers who promoted the product reported losing up to a hundred followers after the “pervert glasses” label went viral.

## Why it matters
The episode underscores the tight coupling between hardware innovation and privacy law. Developers of camera‑equipped wearables now face heightened scrutiny from regulators, civil‑rights groups and a skeptical public. A perception of surveillance can stall adoption, limit market opportunities, and expose companies to lawsuits. For the broader AR ecosystem, the controversy may slow investment until clear consent mechanisms become standard.

## How to think about it
When building wearables with recording capabilities, start with a visible, tamper‑proof indicator that cannot be disabled by software or hardware hacks. Embed consent logs that timestamp each capture and store them locally for a limited period. Design APIs that require explicit user permission before any audio or video stream starts, and provide administrators with remote‑wipe and disable functions. Finally, engage privacy experts early and run public‑beta tests in controlled environments to surface perception issues before full launch.

<Callout type="warning">Do not ship devices that allow the recording indicator to be disabled or hidden; doing so can expose you to legal liability and irreversible brand damage.</Callout>
