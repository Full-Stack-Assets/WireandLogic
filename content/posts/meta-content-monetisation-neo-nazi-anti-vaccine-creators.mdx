Meta’s invitation‑only Content Monetisation programme, which paid almost US$3 billion to 16.2 million creators in 2025, has been found to reward accounts that spread extremist and anti‑vaccine content. An ABC News Verify investigation identified four Australian pages – a neo‑Nazi‑linked agitator, a far‑right news site, an anti‑immigration group, and an anti‑vaccine activist – that have been receiving ad revenue since late 2025. The payments conflict with Meta’s own rules that bar advertising on misleading medical information and restrict certain hate‑based content. This revelation spotlights a gap between Meta’s policy statements and the reality of its monetisation enforcement. Developers building on Meta’s APIs and advertisers need to understand the risk exposure.

<Callout type="takeaway">Meta’s monetisation system is financially rewarding content that breaches its own policy limits.</Callout>

## What happened
An ABC News Verify report examined several Australian Facebook pages and found they were part of Meta’s invitation‑only Content Monetisation programme. Hugo Lennon, a far‑right agitator with neo‑Nazi connections, has been earning revenue since September 2025 after shouting racist abuse at Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi. The Noticer, a website that regularly publishes white‑supremacist material, joined the programme in November 2025, while a page promoting the anti‑immigration group March for Australia registered in December 2025. Monica Smit, founder of the anti‑vaccine group Reignite Democracy Australia, entered the programme in September 2025 and has reportedly earned ad revenue on Facebook since 2017.

Meta disclosed that it paid almost US$3 billion to an estimated 16.2 million monetised accounts in 2025. The investigation highlighted that the identified pages posted content that directly conflicts with Meta’s published monetisation policies, which prohibit advertising on misleading medical information and restrict certain hate‑based content.

## Why it matters
When a platform financially rewards creators who violate its own standards, it creates a feedback loop that amplifies harmful narratives and erodes public trust. Advertisers risk brand‑safety incidents as their ads can appear alongside extremist or misinformation‑laden posts. Policymakers and civil‑society groups may cite these findings to demand greater transparency and stricter enforcement from large tech firms. For developers integrating with Meta’s APIs, the episode underscores the importance of building independent safeguards rather than relying solely on Meta’s internal controls.

## How to think about it
Treat Meta’s monetisation eligibility as a starting point, not a guarantee of compliance. Implement layered content reviews: combine Meta’s brand‑safety tools with manual audits of publisher histories, especially for high‑risk topics like health misinformation or extremist rhetoric. When allocating ad spend, prioritize publishers with clear policy adherence records and consider supplemental third‑party verification services. Finally, stay informed about Meta’s policy updates and be prepared to adjust targeting rules quickly as new investigations emerge.
