Last week the Savannah Police Department in Georgia terminated four sworn officers and two civilian staff after an internal audit revealed they had used the department’s Flock Safety license‑plate reader for personal searches. The misuse included looking up friends, family members, and even granting external law‑enforcement personnel unauthorized access to the system. The cases have been referred to the Georgia Bureau of Investigation for criminal review, and the department has instituted weekly audits to prevent future abuse. This incident highlights the risks of powerful surveillance tools falling into the wrong hands. For developers building or integrating similar systems, the fallout underscores the need for robust governance.

<Callout type="takeaway">Misusing automated license‑plate readers can quickly erode public trust and trigger legal consequences.</Callout>

## What happened
The Savannah Police Department launched an internal investigation on July 27 after routine monitoring of the Flock Safety platform flagged searches that did not align with legitimate law‑enforcement activity. The review identified four sworn officers and two civilian employees who had run queries on personal acquaintances, family members, and in one case, provided an outside officer with system access they were not authorized to have.

All six employees were placed on administrative leave, then terminated, and the Georgia Bureau of Investigation has opened a parallel criminal investigation. The department highlighted that every search in the Flock system is automatically logged with the user, timestamp, and stated reason, and that these logs were instrumental in detecting the abuse. In response, Savannah Police has instituted recurring weekly audits by the Office of Professional Standards and reinforced role‑based access controls.

## Why it matters
License‑plate readers (LPRs) like Flock can dramatically accelerate investigations, but they also collect massive amounts of location data on ordinary citizens. When that data is accessed for personal reasons, it violates privacy expectations and can expose agencies to civil lawsuits and criminal charges. The public’s trust in surveillance technology hinges on transparent policies and enforceable safeguards; breaches erode that trust and can lead to stricter legislative oversight. For developers, the incident is a reminder that building powerful data‑collection tools carries a responsibility to embed auditability, least‑privilege access, and clear usage policies from day one.

## How to think about it
When designing or deploying surveillance APIs, start with a data‑privacy‑first mindset. Define clear, narrow use‑case policies and encode them into the system’s permission model. Require multi‑factor authentication for any query that accesses personally identifiable information, and log the purpose field with a controlled vocabulary that can be validated automatically. Implement real‑time alerts for anomalous patterns—such as repeated searches on the same plate outside of an active investigation—and schedule independent audits at least weekly. Finally, provide mandatory training for all users and enforce disciplinary consequences for violations.

<Callout type="warning">Do not rely solely on post‑hoc audit logs; combine them with real‑time monitoring and automated anomaly detection to catch misuse early.</Callout>
