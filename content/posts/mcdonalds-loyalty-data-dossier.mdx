Last week a California resident exercised their CCPA right and asked McDonald’s for a copy of the data the company stores about them. Within days the fast‑food giant emailed a 515‑page PDF that catalogues every purchase, loyalty points, and targeted offer ever sent. The sheer volume of detail turned a routine data‑access request into a stark illustration of how deeply loyalty programs profile customers. For developers building similar systems, the file is a reminder that data collection can quickly become invasive. Understanding what’s inside helps shape responsible design and compliance strategies.

<Callout type="takeaway">McDonald’s loyalty program collects enough data to build a detailed personal dossier on each user.</Callout>

## What happened
When the author submitted a request through McDonald’s Privacy Rights Center, the company responded with a 515‑page PDF stamped with the golden arches. The document lists every transaction recorded by the app, the specific offers that were pushed, and the accumulation of loyalty points over several years. The author used a generative‑AI tool to extract key sections, then cross‑checked the results against the original file.

Privacy experts note that while the level of detail feels invasive, it is not unique to McDonald’s; many large U.S. retailers compile similarly granular profiles for their loyalty members. Jeff Chester of the Center for Digital Democracy called the practice “commercial surveillance,” and a McDonald’s spokesperson emphasized that the data is used to “provide a more engaging, personal customer experience.”

## Why it matters
The dossier demonstrates how a single loyalty program can amass a comprehensive behavioral portrait, raising red flags under the California Consumer Privacy Act and other emerging regulations. Developers must consider that such data, if breached, could expose purchasing habits, location patterns, and even health‑related choices. Moreover, the ability to algorithmically predict future orders gives companies significant leverage over pricing and promotions, potentially eroding consumer autonomy.

## How to think about it
If you build or maintain a loyalty system, start by mapping every data point you collect and ask whether it directly supports a user‑visible benefit. Implement easy‑to‑use data‑export tools that generate concise, machine‑readable files rather than massive PDFs. Regularly audit retention policies and give users granular controls to delete or limit specific categories of data. Finally, treat any AI‑generated summaries as a first pass—always verify against the raw data before making decisions.

<Callout type="warning">Never share the full 515‑page dossier publicly; it contains sensitive personal and transaction information that could be exploited if exposed.</Callout>
