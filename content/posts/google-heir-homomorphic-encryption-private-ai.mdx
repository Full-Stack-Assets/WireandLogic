Google announced today that its new HEIR compiler is now open‑source, allowing developers to run AI models on encrypted data without ever decrypting it. HEIR translates ordinary code into a form that can be executed under fully homomorphic encryption (FHE), turning privacy‑preserving inference from a research curiosity into a usable tool. The blog post showcases demos that predict health outcomes in 24 seconds and click‑through rates in up to eight minutes on a single CPU core. While the latency is far above cleartext inference, the cost of FHE is dropping and the performance gap is narrowing. This shift could unlock AI services in regulated sectors that previously avoided cloud processing.

<Callout type="takeaway">HEIR makes encrypted AI inference practical by turning cost into the primary trade‑off.</Callout>

## What happened

Google introduced HEIR (Homomorphic Encryption Inference Runtime), an open‑source compiler that takes regular code and emits a version that runs on ciphertexts under FHE. The toolkit joins Google’s existing private‑computing portfolio, which includes differential privacy, private set membership, and secure enclaves. HEIR abstracts the complex mathematics of homomorphic encryption, letting engineers focus on model logic rather than cryptographic details.

In the initial release, the team demonstrated inference on two public benchmarks: a health‑prediction task from the UCI repository completed in 24 seconds, and a click‑prediction workload (Criteo) that took between 228 and 489 seconds on a single‑threaded CPU. Independent Hacker News discussion highlighted primitive operation timings from the TFHE‑rs library—equality checks at ~80 ms, addition/subtraction at ~100 ms, and division at ~8 seconds—illustrating the current performance landscape.

Google also noted ongoing work to accelerate FHE on GPUs, referencing a recent CIFAR demo that runs in roughly 200 ms, suggesting that future hardware support could narrow the latency gap dramatically.

## Why it matters

The ability to compute on encrypted data removes the fundamental trade‑off between data privacy and cloud‑based AI functionality. Sectors such as healthcare, finance, and genomics, which are bound by strict regulations, can now envision deploying powerful models without exposing raw user data. By lowering the engineering barrier through an open‑source compiler, Google accelerates the ecosystem of privacy‑preserving services, potentially reshaping how companies monetize sensitive datasets. However, the current performance overhead—orders of magnitude slower than cleartext inference—means that early adopters must balance privacy gains against latency constraints.

## How to think about it

Start by identifying workloads where privacy is non‑negotiable and latency tolerance is high—batch risk scoring, cohort analysis, or offline recommendation generation. Clone the HEIR repository, rewrite a small model in the supported language, and use the compiler to generate FHE code. Benchmark the encrypted inference against cleartext baselines to quantify the cost trade‑off. If performance is insufficient, explore hybrid designs that keep the most sensitive features encrypted while processing the rest in cleartext, or wait for GPU‑accelerated FHE libraries that are emerging in the community. Contribute performance patches back to the project to help the ecosystem mature.

<Callout type="warning">Do not deploy HEIR‑based models in latency‑sensitive production endpoints until performance meets your service‑level requirements.</Callout>
