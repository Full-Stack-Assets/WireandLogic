When a 1.5 million‑page website started seeing a flood of traffic from unknown sources, the owner quickly realized most of it wasn’t human. Over the past year, bot activity has surged to the point where more than half of all visits are automated, overwhelming analytics and inflating ad spend. The scramble to differentiate real customers from scrapers has turned routine monitoring into a full‑time cat‑and‑mouse game. Understanding how these bots operate and what they cost is essential for anyone running large‑scale web properties.

<Callout type="takeaway">Bots now account for the majority of traffic on large sites, eroding data quality and revenue.</Callout>

## What happened
The site’s 1.5 million pages began attracting bot traffic that, according to Radware’s 2025 Ecommerce Bot Threat Report, made up 57% of total ecommerce visits in 2024, with 31% classified as “bad bots” that scrape content, test stolen cards, or clog carts. Merchants on the same ecosystem reported conversion rates collapsing to 0.02% after a sudden surge from 20 k to 400 k monthly impressions, rendering reporting almost useless.

Further analysis revealed that a single merchant monitoring over 200 stores found 73% of overall traffic to be fake, a figure echoed by Cloudflare data showing 29% of Black Friday 2024 traffic originated from bots and Akamai noting an 18% rise during Cyber Week. Geographic sources spanned China, Brazil, Vietnam, and Nigeria, and bots have become sophisticated enough to mimic real browsers using headless emulators, especially on mobile apps where activity jumped 160% year‑over‑year.

## Why it matters
When bots dominate the traffic stream, key metrics such as conversion rate, bounce rate, and average order value become meaningless, leading teams to make decisions based on polluted data. Advertising budgets are wasted on impressions that never convert, while SEO rankings can suffer if scrapers duplicate content or generate malicious requests. Moreover, the constant need to filter and block malicious traffic adds operational overhead and can expose the infrastructure to denial‑of‑service attacks if not managed properly.

## How to think about it
Start by instrumenting your traffic with a reputable bot‑detection service that tags requests as human, good bot, or bad bot. Layer rate‑limiting at the edge (CDN or WAF) to throttle suspicious IP ranges and enforce request caps per minute. For high‑risk endpoints—login, checkout, API—add challenge mechanisms such as CAPTCHAs or JavaScript challenges that only real browsers can solve. Continuously monitor key conversion funnels and compare bot‑filtered versus raw metrics to spot anomalies early. Finally, maintain an incident‑response playbook that outlines steps for rapid rule updates when new scraper patterns emerge.

<Callout type="warning">Avoid blanket IP blocks; they can unintentionally cut off legitimate traffic from regions with shared cloud providers.</Callout>
