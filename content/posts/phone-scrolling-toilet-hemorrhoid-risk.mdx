If you habitually scroll on your phone while sitting on the toilet, you may be doing more than just killing time. A recent PLOS One study of 125 adults found that users who bring their smartphones into the bathroom spend significantly longer on the loo. Longer sitting translates into higher pressure on the rectal veins, a known pathway to hemorrhoids. Beyond the medical angle, the habit also raises hygiene concerns that affect anyone who shares a bathroom. Understanding the evidence helps developers and builders design healthier spaces and digital experiences.
<Callout type="takeaway">Using a phone on the toilet prolongs sitting time and raises hemorrhoid risk.</Callout>
## What happened
The study recruited 125 adults undergoing screening colonoscopies and asked them about bathroom habits, phone use, fiber intake, and exercise. Sixty‑six percent reported regularly using a smartphone while on the toilet, and those users spent a statistically significant amount of time longer than participants who left their phones outside. Endoscopic exams confirmed a higher prevalence of hemorrhoids among the phone‑using group. About 54% of respondents used their phones to read news and 44% for social media during the visit.
Other coverage reinforces these findings. A gastroenterologist quoted in The Guardian warned that phone use can increase the time spent on the toilet, leading to greater strain on the rectum and a higher chance of piles, anal fissures, or even rectal prolapse. Healthline notes that phones are often dirtier than toilet seats, harboring bacteria such as E. coli, which adds a hygiene risk for anyone sharing the space.
## Why it matters
The health implications are clear: prolonged sitting on the toilet raises intra‑abdominal pressure, a primary factor in hemorrhoid formation. For developers, this underscores the importance of designing bathroom experiences that discourage extended occupancy—think of sensor‑activated lighting or subtle timers that remind users to finish promptly. For builders, incorporating easy‑to‑clean surfaces and hand‑washing stations can mitigate the bacterial transfer from phones to skin. On a personal level, the habit can erode productivity; minutes lost to scrolling add up over weeks, and the discomfort of hemorrhoids can affect focus and comfort at work.

## How to think about it
Treat the bathroom as a brief, purpose‑specific pause rather than a digital lounge. Set a timer on your watch or use a bathroom‑friendly app that automatically locks after a few minutes. If you need to read, consider offline content—download articles or podcasts before you go. For shared spaces, keep phones stored outside the stall and use a waterproof speaker for audio. Finally, incorporate these habits into broader wellness routines: stand up, stretch, and move after you finish to reduce pressure on the rectal veins.
<Callout type="warning">Avoid keeping your phone in the bathroom for extended periods; the device can become a vector for fecal bacteria and increase the risk of infection.</Callout>
