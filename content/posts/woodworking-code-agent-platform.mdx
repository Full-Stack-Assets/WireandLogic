Imagine ordering a custom bed frame the way you order software. A recent Ask HN thread revealed a prototype platform where agents turn woodworking tasks into YAML‑driven procedures, generate cut‑order DAGs, and export CNC‑ready files. The system lets users describe designs in plain language, pick join primitives, and even preview the build in AR. If the vision holds, the line between code and craft could blur dramatically.

<Callout type="takeaway">Agent‑driven parametric tooling lets builders treat wood projects like code, accelerating iteration and reducing manual errors.</Callout>

## What happened
The discussion introduced a platform that treats every operation as YAML, allowing agents to quickly adopt new procedures and even file feature requests when a needed tool is missing. Users can export to 3D‑printing files, video walkthroughs, STEP files, obtain a full BOM, and generate a cut plan that is represented as a directed‑acyclic graph derived from geometric constraints.

Beyond basic cuts, the system supports a library of join primitives—rabbets, dados, dovetails, pocket screws—and lets agents experiment with them digitally against existing designs. Tool availability influences which joins are feasible, and the platform can render the project at life‑size in AR for final inspection.

The author expressed skepticism about fully automating woodworking with LLMs, noting that achieving reliable, adoptable automation would be a massive breakthrough.

## Why it matters
By converting physical joinery into code, the platform lowers the barrier for software engineers to prototype high‑quality furniture without deep carpentry expertise. It also streamlines supply‑chain interactions, turning a design into a real‑world BOM that can be sent directly to lumberyards. If adopted broadly, makers could iterate faster, reduce material waste, and bring custom, high‑end pieces to market at costs previously reserved for mass‑produced items.

## How to think about it
Start by modeling simple parts in YAML and let the agent generate a cut plan; validate the output against your machine’s toolpaths before committing to material. Use the built‑in library of join primitives to explore alternatives, and iterate through natural‑language prompts to refine dimensions or materials. Always cross‑check the generated BOM and AR preview with real‑world constraints such as grain direction and tool availability.

<Callout type="warning">Never ship a piece based solely on AI‑generated files; always verify dimensions and tool paths before cutting.</Callout>
