In September 2022 the UK government unveiled a “mini‑budget” that slashed taxes for the highest earners without offsetting revenue. The move, championed by Prime Minister Liz Truss and Chancellor Kwasi Kwarteng, sent bond yields soaring and forced a rapid policy reversal. Economists quickly pointed to a new LSE working paper that examined five decades of similar cuts across 18 rich nations. The study found that while the wealthy’s after‑tax income rose, unemployment and GDP growth remained unchanged. For developers and builders, the episode illustrates how macro‑policy can shift market confidence without delivering the promised hiring surge.

<Callout type="takeaway">Tax cuts for the rich raise their income but do not spur growth or jobs.</Callout>

## What happened
The “mini‑budget” announced in September 2022 reduced income tax thresholds and eliminated the top‑rate surcharge, relying on unfunded revenue cuts. Markets reacted violently: the pound fell sharply, gilt yields spiked, and the Bank of England intervened to stabilize the bond market. Within weeks the government reversed most measures under intense political pressure.

A working paper by David Hope and Julian Limberg, first released in 2020, aggregated data from 18 advanced economies over the past fifty years. Their analysis showed that major tax reductions for the rich consistently increased after‑tax earnings for that group, while macro‑indicators such as unemployment rates and real GDP growth showed no statistically significant change.

## Why it matters
The findings debunk the classic “trickle‑down” promise that wealthier taxpayers will reinvest savings into hiring and innovation. For the tech sector, the lack of a growth boost means that lower‑tax policies do not guarantee a larger pool of skilled workers or higher project budgets. Policymakers and investors should therefore treat tax‑cut proposals as political signals rather than reliable levers for economic expansion.

## How to think about it
When evaluating any tax‑cut proposal, start with the empirical record rather than rhetoric. Compare the policy to peer‑country case studies and ask whether the fiscal gap will be closed through spending cuts or borrowing. For development teams, focus on cash‑flow forecasts and hiring pipelines that are grounded in actual demand, not assumed tax‑driven spending. Align budgeting cycles with macro‑economic indicators rather than political announcements.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that cutting taxes for high earners will automatically lower your payroll costs or expand your talent pool.</Callout>
