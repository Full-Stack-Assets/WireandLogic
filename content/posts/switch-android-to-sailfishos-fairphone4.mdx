After growing frustrated with Google’s tightening grip on Android, I decided to replace the OS on my Fairphone 4 with a Linux‑based system. I settled on SailfishOS, a community‑driven platform that runs natively on the device and offers full SSH access for tinkering. The switch exposed both the promise of a truly open mobile stack and the rough edges that still keep most users on Android. Understanding these trade‑offs is crucial for anyone considering a move to mobile Linux.

<Callout type="takeaway">SailfishOS proves you can run a functional Linux phone, but you must accept missing features and occasional instability.</Callout>

## What happened

For months I have watched Google layer more proprietary services onto AOSP—mandatory Play Services, AI‑driven features, and the recent removal of user‑chosen app installation—turning Android into a series of “paper cuts.” The cumulative friction convinced me to look for a truly open alternative.

I evaluated Ubuntu Touch, postmarketOS, and SailfishOS, checking each against the Fairphone 4’s hardware support. SailfishOS offered the most complete port, a gesture‑based UI, and the ability to SSH into the phone over USB or Wi‑Fi, which fit my tinkering workflow.

The experience is solid overall, but the Fairphone port ships with outdated Python and glibc versions, and Waydroid and GPS are currently broken. Community‑built apps range from useful to half‑finished, such as a WhatsApp client that I still rely on.

## Why it matters

The switch highlights both the viability and the current limits of mobile Linux. A functional Linux phone gives users control over updates, privacy, and the software stack, challenging the OEM‑driven lock‑in that dominates smartphones. At the same time, missing hardware support and immature app ecosystems keep the majority of consumers on Android or iOS, slowing broader adoption of open mobile platforms.

## How to think about it

Start by confirming that your device has a maintained Linux port and that the essential hardware—camera, GPS, cellular—works out of the box. List the apps you cannot live without and test their Linux equivalents or community ports before committing. Keep a fallback Android device for banking, GPS‑reliant services, and any proprietary peripherals. Use SSH for regular maintenance and consider dual‑booting if you need occasional Android functionality. Stay engaged with the distro’s forum to track fixes and contribute patches where possible.

<Callout type="warning">Do not rely on this setup as your primary banking or navigation device until GPS and secure payment integrations are proven stable.</Callout>
