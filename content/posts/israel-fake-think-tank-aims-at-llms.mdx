The Hanover Institute for Public Policy appeared this week as a new think‑tank focused on Israel‑Palestine issues. Within seven days it posted more than 100 reports that read like neutral policy analyses but were crafted to be cited by AI chatbots such as Claude and Gemini. The institute’s disclaimer reveals it was created by the Israeli Government Advertising Agency through the PR firm Piro, Inc. By engineering content that LLMs treat as credible, the operation seeks to shape the information fed to users of generative AI. The discovery highlights a new frontier in state‑run influence campaigns.

<Callout type="takeaway">State actors are now building fake think tanks to poison large language models with tailored narratives.</Callout>

## What happened
The Hanover Institute’s website mimics a legitimate policy organization, publishing reports on questions like “Does AIPAC Use ‘Dark Money’ in Elections?” and “Is Israel Carrying out a Deliberate Campaign of Starvation in Gaza?” In just over a week the site released at least 100 such articles, each formatted with footnotes, tables of contents, and a neutral tone designed to appeal to LLMs.

The institute’s about page discloses that it was created on behalf of the Israeli Government Advertising Agency by Piro, Inc., a firm co‑founded by Daniel Rosenberg. Piro describes its service as “AI Story Optimization,” explicitly engineering content for how large language models evaluate credibility. Analysts refer to this practice as “LLM poisoning,” a form of influence that targets the training and retrieval pipelines of chat‑based AI.

## Why it matters
Influencing LLMs differs from traditional propaganda because the output reaches millions of users instantly and can be cited as factual by the model itself. When a chatbot draws on these fabricated reports, it may reinforce pro‑Israeli narratives or downplay criticism, subtly shaping public perception without a human editor’s bias filter. The operation also demonstrates how state actors can weaponize the credibility heuristics of AI, raising concerns for developers, policymakers, and users who rely on LLMs for unbiased information.

## How to think about it
When evaluating AI‑generated answers, trace the cited sources back to their original publishers. Treat any report that lacks author attribution or clear institutional backing as low‑confidence, especially if it aligns closely with a geopolitical agenda. Developers should incorporate provenance checks and flag content that originates from domains with known influence operations. For policymakers, consider regulations that require disclosure of state‑funded AI content creation.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume every neutral‑toned policy report is malicious; verify authorship and funding before labeling it as disinformation.</Callout>
