Illinois has just enacted the Children's Online Social Media Safety Act, compelling every operating system sold or used in the state to report a child's age bracket at account creation. By January 1 2028, device manufacturers must present a birth‑date field and translate it into one of four brackets—under 13, 13‑15, 16‑17, or 18 and up. Apps can then query an OS‑provided API to receive that bracket and automatically enable safety settings for minors. The move promises uniform child‑safety controls but also raises significant privacy and implementation challenges for developers.

<Callout type="takeaway">OS‑level age signals will become the default gatekeeper for child‑safety features, shifting compliance burdens onto platform providers.</Callout>

## What happened
Illinois Governor JB Pritzker signed HB5511, the Children’s Online Social Media Safety Act, into law after an 82‑27 House vote and a unanimous 57‑0 Senate vote. The statute obliges “covered manufacturers” – device makers, OS providers, and app stores – to add an age‑verification interface at account setup and to encrypt all related signals. By January 1 2028 the interface must collect a birth date, map it to one of four brackets, and expose the bracket via an API that apps can call after July 1 2028. Violations may incur fines up to $50,000 per infraction, though good‑faith errors are exempt.

The approach mirrors earlier efforts in Colorado (SB26‑051) and California (AB‑1043), which also rely on OS‑level age signals. Those states initially excluded open‑source platforms, prompting pushback from Linux vendors; Colorado later adjusted its language after lobbying from System76. Illinois’ bill does not contain an explicit open‑source carve‑out, leaving developers of non‑proprietary OSes to interpret compliance.

## Why it matters
The law creates a uniform technical layer for child‑safety, meaning app developers can rely on a single API instead of implementing disparate age checks. However, it also introduces a new data‑collection point that could be exploited for profiling or targeted advertising, especially if signals are combined with other identifiers. Platform providers must redesign onboarding flows, update privacy policies, and ensure end‑to‑end encryption, while open‑source projects may face legal uncertainty without clear exemptions.

## How to think about it
1. **Audit your onboarding flow** – Ensure you can accept an optional age‑bracket from the OS and map it to your internal safety settings.
2. **Implement graceful fallback** – If the OS signal is unavailable or erroneous, retain your existing age‑verification mechanism rather than assuming compliance.
3. **Review data handling** – Store only the bracket, not the raw birth date, and treat it as sensitive personal information under state law.
4. **Monitor open‑source discussions** – Follow any legislative clarifications or community‑driven implementations that address Linux and other non‑proprietary platforms.
5. **Prepare for audits** – Log API calls securely and be ready to demonstrate good‑faith compliance if the Attorney General investigates.

<Callout type="warning">Do not expose the raw birth‑date or combine the age bracket with persistent identifiers without explicit user consent; doing so could violate Illinois privacy statutes and attract hefty fines.</Callout>
