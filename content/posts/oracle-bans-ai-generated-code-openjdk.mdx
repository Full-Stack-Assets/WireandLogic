Oracle’s OpenJDK governing board announced an interim policy that outright bans contributions generated by large language models or other generative AI systems. The rule applies to source code, documentation, and any other content submitted to OpenJDK repositories, pull requests, or issue trackers. Oracle says the ban is intended to protect reviewer capacity, security of the JDK, and clear intellectual‑property ownership. The decision arrives as other Oracle‑backed projects, such as GraalVM, continue to allow AI‑assisted contributions, highlighting a split strategy within the company. Developers who rely on AI tools now face a new compliance hurdle when working on the Java platform.

<Callout type="takeaway">Oracle’s new policy blocks AI‑generated code in OpenJDK to safeguard review workload, security, and IP clarity.</Callout>

## What happened
In early April 2026 OpenJDK published an interim policy that broadly bans any content created, in part or in whole, by large language models, diffusion models, or similar deep‑learning systems. The ban covers source code, text, images, and any material in Git repositories, pull‑request discussions, email threads, wiki pages, or JBS issues.

The governing board listed three reasons: reviewer burden from plausible but incorrect AI output, the safety and security of the JDK that underpins mission‑critical systems, and intellectual‑property uncertainty because the Oracle Contributor Agreement requires contributors to own the rights they grant, which is unclear for AI‑generated material. Contributors may still use AI for private debugging, research, or to understand existing code, but any contribution that includes AI‑generated lines—even if edited—is prohibited.

To enforce the rule, OpenJDK’s automated review system Skara now requires a checkbox confirming compliance before a pull request can be merged. The policy also clarifies that non‑LLM tools such as spell‑checkers, grammar‑checkers, auto‑completion, and refactoring features are allowed, provided they do not rely on large language models.

## Why it matters
The Java platform powers billions of devices and countless enterprise applications. Introducing AI‑generated code could accelerate development but also risks inserting subtle bugs that are hard to detect, increasing the burden on a limited pool of reviewers. By prohibiting such contributions, Oracle aims to preserve the reliability and security expectations of Java users.

At the same time, the policy creates a compliance challenge for developers and organizations that have integrated AI assistants into their workflow. Teams must now audit every contribution for AI involvement, potentially slowing release cycles and adding legal overhead. The split between OpenJDK’s strict stance and GraalVM’s permissive policy may also fragment the open‑source Java ecosystem, forcing contributors to choose where to invest their effort.

## How to think about it
Treat the policy as a binary gate: any code you submit to OpenJDK must be wholly human‑written. If you use an AI assistant, ensure you rewrite the suggestion line‑by‑line and remove any trace of AI origin before committing. Incorporate a verification step in your CI pipeline that checks the Skara compliance checkbox and logs the provenance of each change. When in doubt, err on the side of manual implementation or seek a review from a senior maintainer before opening a pull request.

<Callout type="warning">Submitting even a single AI‑generated line can violate the policy and result in contribution rejection or legal exposure under the Oracle Contributor Agreement.</Callout>

## FAQ

GraalVM’s Coding Assistants policy permits AI‑generated contributions under the same Oracle Contributor Agreement, whereas OpenJDK’s interim policy bans them entirely, reflecting a divergent risk assessment within Oracle‑backed projects.
