The Nashville Metro Council voted 27‑5 to give the city the power to acquire a 23‑acre parcel slated for a $700 million data center next to the zoo. The legislation, championed by Mayor Freddie O’Connell, authorizes eminent domain if negotiations with developer DC Blox fall short. Opponents, including zoo officials and celebrities, argue the facility could disturb animals and strain local resources. For developers, the move signals that large‑scale infrastructure projects can face unprecedented municipal pushback. Understanding the legal and community landscape is now essential for anyone planning data center builds.

<Callout type="takeaway">Eminent domain can become a decisive tool for municipalities opposing data center projects near sensitive sites.</Callout>

## What happened
By a 27‑5 vote, the Metro Council approved a proposal that would let the city negotiate a purchase of the 23‑acre site owned by DC Blox, and if talks fail, seize it through eminent domain. The developer bought the property for about $23 million and plans a single‑story, 69,220‑square‑foot facility costing more than $700 million.

Zoo officials and a petition that gathered over 500,000 signatures have voiced concerns about noise, light, and vibrations that could affect animals, especially hearing‑sensitive species like clouded leopards. Celebrities such as Brad Paisley, Sheryl Crow and Jack White have publicly opposed the project.

The city would need to pay at least the market value of roughly $37.4 million for the land, a figure higher than DC Blox’s purchase price, using funds from the general budget. The legislation does not compel the city to take the property, but it equips officials with a powerful tool should negotiations stall.

## Why it matters
The episode illustrates how data center developers must now factor municipal authority and community activism into project risk assessments. Eminent‑domain actions, traditionally reserved for utilities or transportation, are being repurposed to protect environmental and public‑interest concerns, potentially inflating acquisition costs and extending timelines. Cities may also impose zoning restrictions or moratoria, as Nashville recently did, creating additional layers of regulatory uncertainty. For builders, overlooking these dynamics can jeopardize financing, delay construction, or even halt a project entirely.

## How to think about it
When evaluating a data center site, incorporate a political‑risk matrix that scores zoning flexibility, community sentiment, and the likelihood of eminent‑domain exposure. Conduct early environmental impact assessments that address noise and vibration, especially if the site borders sensitive facilities like zoos or hospitals. Build contingency budgets for possible land‑acquisition premiums or legal fees. Finally, maintain transparent dialogue with local officials and residents to identify mitigation strategies before formal approvals are sought.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that a signed purchase agreement guarantees project continuity; municipal tools like eminent domain can override private contracts.</Callout>
