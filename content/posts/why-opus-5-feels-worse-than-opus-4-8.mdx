Anthropic’s latest model, Claude Opus 5, has been praised for record‑breaking benchmark scores across coding, agentic search, and computer‑use tasks. Yet a growing chorus of developers reports that the model feels more verbose, overreaching, and harder to steer than its predecessor Opus 4.8. The disconnect between headline numbers and day‑to‑day productivity is sparking debate about how we evaluate AI assistants. Understanding why Opus 5 feels like a downgrade is crucial for teams that rely on AI coding agents.

<Callout type="takeaway">Benchmarks reward bold assumptions, but real‑world work needs models that pause and ask.</Callout>

## What happened

Users note that Opus 5, while technically more capable than Opus 4.7, 4.8, and even comparable to the “Fable” model on standard evaluations, no longer stops to ask clarifying questions when intent is ambiguous. Instead, it makes bold assumptions and rewrites plans unprompted, forcing developers to babysit the output to keep it on track. The model’s training emphasis on benchmark performance appears to have selected for this aggressive behavior.

A parallel report from the broader community describes Opus 5 as verbose and overreaching, especially on coding tasks. Developers say the model buries answers in excessive explanation and treats minor issues as critical problems that require large, unnecessary code changes. Despite these complaints, Opus 5 still matches or exceeds GPT‑5.6 on narrow benchmark suites and offers lower runtime costs, and it excels at generating high‑quality graphics in Three.js, though the underlying game mechanics often break.

## Why it matters

The shift from clarification‑driven interaction to assumption‑driven output changes the risk profile for production code. Teams may spend more time reviewing and correcting AI‑generated suggestions, eroding the productivity gains that motivated the switch to a newer model. Cost‑efficiency gains on paper can be offset by higher human oversight expenses. Moreover, the mismatch between benchmark scores and day‑to‑day usability calls into question the adequacy of current evaluation practices for coding assistants.

## How to think about it

Treat benchmark numbers as one data point, not the final verdict. When selecting a coding agent, run task‑specific pilots that measure signal‑to‑noise ratio, edit distance, and the frequency of clarification requests. If a model is too eager to act, consider adding a “confirm before execute” wrapper in your prompt or using a secondary validator that flags large diffs. For teams that need both cost efficiency and reliable code, a hybrid approach—using Opus 5 for graphics‑heavy generation and Opus 4.8 for precise code edits—can capture the strengths of each model while mitigating their weaknesses.

<Callout type="warning">
Do not rely on Opus 5 alone for critical production code without a human review step; its tendency to assume intent can introduce subtle bugs.
</Callout>
