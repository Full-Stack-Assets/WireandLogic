Developers worldwide have been buzzing about Dmitry Grinberg’s recent critique of RISC‑V, which landed on the front page of Hacker News. A hardware engineer based in Trinidad and Tobago pushed back, highlighting how shipping costs and customs duties turn cheap chips into expensive projects. For educators and hobbyists in regions without free‑shipping options, those extra dollars can mean the difference between a hands‑on class and a lecture slide. The debate isn’t just technical—it’s about accessibility and real‑world adoption.

<Callout type="takeaway">RISC‑V’s real‑world adoption hinges as much on affordable, accessible hardware as on its technical merits.</Callout>

## What happened
The original article by Dmitry Grinberg criticized RISC‑V’s design choices, such as compressed store offsets and the optional Zicsr extension, arguing they add unnecessary complexity. His piece sparked a heated discussion on Hacker News and Lobsters, becoming the most substantial criticism of the ISA in recent memory. In response, a third‑world embedded engineer from Trinidad and Tobago detailed how those technical debates overlook a fundamental barrier: the cost and logistics of obtaining RISC‑V development boards.

He explained that while a chip may cost a dollar in the United States, shipping to the Caribbean can add $60‑$200, and customs fees often double the expense. Local sponsors and PCB manufacturers frequently decline partnerships because they cannot justify shipping tiny components to remote islands. This price inflation directly limits the ability of teachers to provide each student with a board, reducing hands‑on learning to a single demo unit.

The engineer also noted that similar challenges affect peers in Nigeria, Bangladesh, and other regions where “free shipping” is a non‑existent concept, turning nominal price differences into significant educational inequities.

## Why it matters
When hardware costs become prohibitive, the promise of an open ISA like RISC‑V stalls at the classroom door. Educators in low‑resource settings may revert to legacy platforms or abandon modern curricula altogether, slowing the diffusion of cutting‑edge skills. Moreover, manufacturers miss out on a global market segment that could drive volume economies if logistics were addressed. The debate thus shifts from pure architecture to supply‑chain inclusivity, influencing both community growth and the long‑term viability of RISC‑V ecosystems.

## How to think about it
When evaluating RISC‑V for a project in a cost‑sensitive environment, start by calculating the total cost of ownership: part price, shipping, customs, and any required accessories. Look for regional distributors or bulk‑order programs that can amortize shipping across many units. If logistics remain prohibitive, consider hybrid approaches—using locally sourced microcontrollers for early labs and reserving RISC‑V boards for advanced modules. Engage with the open‑source community to share bulk‑order opportunities and to request documentation that emphasizes low‑cost deployment scenarios.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume “free shipping” or negligible customs fees; always factor in the full landed cost before committing to a hardware platform.</Callout>
