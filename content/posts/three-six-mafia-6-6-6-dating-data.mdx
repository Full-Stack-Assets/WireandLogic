Developers love metrics, and the online “6‑6‑6” dating rule is a perfect case study of how simple numbers can dominate personal decisions. The rule claims a man must score at least a 6 in height, income, and penis size to be considered date‑able. Recent discussions on Hacker News and a personal essay have surfaced raw statistics behind each metric, from height percentiles to earnings data. Understanding the actual distribution of these attributes reveals how restrictive the rule really is and why it matters for product designers building dating platforms.

<Callout type="takeaway">The 6‑6‑6 rule eliminates over 90% of men from the dating pool when applied strictly.</Callout>

## What happened
A six‑foot height sits at roughly the 75th percentile for American men aged 20‑30, meaning only about one quarter of that cohort meets the height threshold. The broader adult male population shows that 91% are below six feet, so the rule instantly discards the vast majority.

Penile length follows a normal distribution with a mean of 5.166 inches and a standard deviation of 0.654 inches. A six‑inch measurement lands at the 90th percentile, leaving just 10% of men in the “size” category.

Income data from the U.S. Census indicates that households earning over $99,999 rose from 34.5% in 2014 to 42.8% in 2024. The discussion also cites a rough equivalence: each additional inch of height is associated with a $25,000 shift in annual salary, while each extra inch of penis length corresponds to a $128,000 salary offset.

## Why it matters
When a dating platform adopts hard cutoffs based on these three metrics, it excludes more than nine out of ten potential male users, dramatically shrinking the active pool and skewing match algorithms. Such exclusion reinforces narrow stereotypes, reduces diversity, and can lead to lower engagement for both sides of the market. Moreover, the underlying data sources vary in reliability—height and penis size are derived from self‑reported studies, while income figures mix household and personal earnings—making any strict rule scientifically shaky.

## How to think about it
Treat the 6‑6‑6 numbers as soft signals rather than hard thresholds. Implement a weighted scoring system where each attribute contributes to an overall compatibility score, allowing users to adjust the importance of height, income, or size. Offer opt‑in filters so users who care about a metric can apply it without forcing everyone into the same mold. Always surface confidence intervals or data provenance so users understand the uncertainty behind each figure.

<Callout type="warning">Do not hard‑code the 6‑6‑6 thresholds into matchmaking logic; doing so can violate anti‑discrimination policies and alienate the majority of users.</Callout>
